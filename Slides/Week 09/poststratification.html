How should you re-weight this if the goal is to analyze the overall views of people in the DMV area?

Additional adjustments

What happens if you still see a discrepancy between the sample and the population even after you’ve adjusted for different probabilities of selection and non-response?

Possible reasons:

  • Random sampling error
  • Coverage error
  • Non-response not fully accounted for by non-response adjustment

Calibration

Calibrations are additional adjustments made to a survey after collection usually to force a sample to resemble a “benchmark” population total.

The basic adjustment is simple: if women are 51% of the target population but 50% of our sample, then we would give them a weight of \(.51/.50 = 1.02\) (and men would get a weight of \(.98\))

Then we would multiply these with our other weights to get a final calibrated weight.

Sources of auxiliary information

  • Those benchmarks need to be reliable estimates of the population values. The Census and American Community Survey (ACS) are two widely used sources.

Calibration for multiple variables

What if we wanted to adjust for gender, ethnicity, education, age, and urban/rural residence?

This poses some potential problems:

  • Even if we make everything dichotomous, that would give us \(2^5 = 36\) combinations. There’s a good chance that we would have little or no data for these combinations.

  • Commonly used sources like the ACS typically publish marginal totals (aggregate counts by group), so we may not be able to find reliable population totals for all of these combinations of characteristics.

Calibration with raking

Raking (or iterative proportional fitting) is a method of calibration that only requires marginal totals.

  • Each calibration variable is weighted separately, one at a time.
  • This process is repeated until the weights (hopefully) stabilize.
  • Usually, weights with extreme values are “trimmed”, which trades some bias for reduced sampling variance.

Post-stratification

  • If we do have data on the joint distributions of all the variables, and we have sufficient data for each category, we can post-stratify instead.

Multilevel Regression with Post-stratification

MRP used for:

  • Adjusting highly non-representative surveys (such as online opt-in polls)
  • Small area estimation (using a national poll to make an inference about a state)

Multilevel Regression with Post-stratification

Basic process:

  • Use survey results to model individual-level predictive model of public opinion for different subgroups (e.g., predicted voted choice for a college educated white woman in Michigan)

  • Generate weighted predictions based on the known frequency of those subgroups in the target population.

  • Multilevel modeling is used in the first step to handle the problem of having limited or no data for certain combinations of predictors. (where data are limited, multilevel models shrink estimates toward the grand mean)

Multilevel Regression with Post-stratification

  • [Gelman et al. (2016)] used an online opt-in poll of Xbox Live players to predict vote intentions in the 2012 election.

  • MRP adjusted estimates closely tracked the national polls. (Raking wasn’t able to achieve this level of accuracy)

https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/high_frequency_polling.pdf

Multilevel Regression with Post-stratification

  • MRP requires building a model for every survey question, and it requires joint (not marginal) distributions for the values in the target population.

  • We don’t have good data on some of the most important predictors, so MRP mostly relies on demographic data.
